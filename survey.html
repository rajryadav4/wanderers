Sky Survey · NEOWISE 2014–2024 · automated search

Billions of infrared detections. Which ones move?

NASA's NEOWISE telescope photographed every part of the sky about every six months for ten years. We cut the sky into 12,288 equal tiles and, tile by tile, a program links each source's detections across the years, measures how far and how steadily it moves, throws out artefacts, and checks ESA's Gaia catalogue: a mover that Gaia already knows is explained. Whatever is left is ranked, so people can look at the most promising candidates first. A candidate is a lead, never a discovery.

    03

    Ranked for people to investigate

    The candidates

    Highest score first. The score rewards no Gaia counterpart, a clean straight track over many years and a red colour (cold brown dwarfs are red in W1−W2), and penalises the opposite. Open a candidate to see its track and check it yourself.

    Hide

    High-confidence keeps movers seen in at least 12 visits whose motion is measured at 10σ or better, and hides those at a tile edge, in a crowded spot, saturated, or classed as likely artefacts. Red is a hint, not proof: cold brown dwarfs are red in W1−W2 because methane absorbs their light in W1 (3.4 µm). Filters and sort are kept in the address, so a shared link shows the same list.

    04

    Honest limits

    What this search cannot tell you

      • "Unexplained" means only that Gaia DR3 has no matching moving star. Many such objects are already known from other surveys (brown dwarfs are too faint and cold for Gaia), and some are artefacts the cuts missed.
      • Only people looking at the images, then follow-up observations, can confirm anything new.

        Look it up in other archives

          It moves, so each link uses the right date: sky viewers open where it should be today (moved with its measured motion), and catalogue searches use its J2000 position (SIMBAD, VizieR) or its 2016.0 position (Gaia DR3), with a radius that covers the uncertainty of that move plus a year of motion. Older images show it earlier along its path, and a few catalogue entries are for their own observing date: the wider SIMBAD search above covers its whole path since 2000.
